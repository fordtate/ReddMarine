## Problem Statement

Private residential docks get power for lighting and accessories, but the electrical
side is an afterthought: no fault monitoring, no diagnostics, no remote control, and
no service relationship after the electrician leaves. Two gaps:

1. **Safety.** Current leaking into the water around an energized dock causes Electric
   Shock Drowning (ESD) — a real, documented, occasionally fatal hazard. Marinas are
   increasingly required to have ground-fault protection (NEC 555). Private residential
   docks are the regulatory gap: often no inspection, no enforcement, no insurance
   requirement.
2. **Control.** Dock lighting (running, spreader, underwater) and accessory loads
   (fridge, speakers, outlets, boat charger) are controlled by house switches or
   nothing. No presets, no remote, no scenes.

The product: one power box per dock that (a) **requires and monitors a certified ELCI
protective breaker added at install** — logging and alerting on residual/leakage
current and fault behavior — and (b) provides networked relay control of lighting and
accessory loads with app presets and remote access. Sold as a hardware unit plus a
recurring subscription; the subscription pays a royalty to the installing dock builder
(if registered in the app) and feeds diagnostics back to that builder for proactive
maintenance and service requests.

## Demand Evidence

**Current strongest evidence: founder conviction + the ESD safety case.** No paying
pre-orders, no signed builder LOIs, no named customer commitments. This is the honest
pre-product starting point.

The founder's read on the status quo: high-end private docks today get "nothing beyond
a standard breaker" for both safety and control.

**Why this is a risk, not a green light:** if owners routinely spend $50k–$200k on a
dock and $0 on dock electrical safety or control, revealed willingness-to-pay for this
specific thing is unproven and probably low. People do not buy what they are not
scared of, and ESD is an invisible risk on their own property.

**The bet that makes it work:** this is a distribution problem, not a demand problem.
Owners do not shop for their own dock hardware; the builder puts it on the quote. If
the builder attaches it as "the dock electrical package," owners take it. That makes
the builder royalty the core thesis, not a growth tactic bolted on the side.

## Business Shape (added by /plan-ceo-review)

The doc assumes a homeowner SaaS. The unit economics (~$5.75/mo gross per dock,
before opex) and the small annual market of high-end private docks fit a
**regional electrical-infrastructure / trade business** — hardware margin +
installed base + builder service contracts, with software as the moat — better
than they fit a venture-scale SaaS. These are different companies: different
funding (revenue-financed vs venture), different staffing (electricians +
ops vs engineers + growth), different exit.

**This is not resolved yet — it is a question the 30-day builder interviews must
answer.** Ask builders directly: do they want to *resell a subscription* to their
client, or do they want to *buy the kit at a margin and run their own service
plan*? Their answer picks the shape. Until then, treat the $15/mo homeowner sub
as one hypothesis, not the plan.

What changes under each answer: **SaaS** → this doc stands as written. **Hybrid**
→ same, plus a "buy at margin" option in the builder agreement. **Trade business**
→ the royalty/subscription model, the unit-economics section, and the 90-day
pricing work all need rework (homeowner sub becomes optional; revenue shifts to
kit margin + a builder platform fee; north-star becomes active-builder count). A
clear trade-business answer triggers a revision of this doc before any 90-day work.

## Status Quo

- **Safety:** typically a standard breaker at the house panel. Sometimes a GFCI outlet.
  Rarely a marine ELCI (30 mA) breaker at the feed, and even then with no monitoring or
  alerting. Owner is usually not thinking about ESD at all.
- **Control:** house light switches, occasionally a consumer smart-plug / Lutron / Sonos
  patchwork, occasionally a high-end integrator (Control4-class) bespoke job where the
  integrator — not the builder — owns the relationship.

## Target User & Narrowest Wedge

**Buyer the business lives or dies on:** the dock builder / marine contractor. They add
it to the build quote; one builder relationship is many docks; the royalty gives them
an install incentive. The waterfront homeowner is the beneficiary (safety + app) but
will not seek it out. Insurers and regulation are a possible tailwind, not a plan.

**Wedge (v1):** ship a **safety-monitoring + lighting-control bundle as a software +
off-the-shelf-parts system** — not a custom-manufactured box — so demand and
subscription-renewal economics get tested on real docks before hardware capital is
spent. Lead the pitch with the safety record + builder diagnostics dashboard; lighting
presets ride the same relay channels.

## Working Assumptions (illustrative, to be validated — see Open Questions)

- Subscription price: **$15/mo** to the homeowner (placeholder for pricing research).
- Builder royalty: **25% of subscription = ~$3.75/mo per active dock**. The royalty
  only becomes attention-worthy income for a builder above roughly **50 cumulative
  installed docks on the system**. This is the central weakness of the model and the
  first thing to pressure-test with real builders.
- **Unit economics per active dock (illustrative):** $15 sub − $3.75 royalty −
  ~$5.50 LTE data ≈ **$5.75/mo gross**, and cloud hosting, payment fees, support
  labor, hardware amortization, and product-liability insurance all come out of that
  $5.75. The model may be barely positive per dock. Either the price is higher than
  $15, the royalty is lower or capped, connectivity is cheaper than LTE, or the
  economics only work at scale. This is a gating question, not a footnote.
- Pilot bar: **3 builders to a paid pilot commitment.** Concretely, a builder pays a
  **pilot fee of ~$500–1,500** — covering one or two kits at/near cost plus install
  support — under a short written agreement. The point is skin in the game, not
  revenue. A verbal "sure, sounds good" does not count.

## Constraints

- **Life-safety regulatory domain on the safety half.** UL 943 (GFCI), UL 1053
  (ground-fault sensing), ABYC E-11, NEC 555. Building a certified protective device is
  an 18–24 month, six-figure testing path. v1 explicitly avoids that path — the box is
  a monitor sitting alongside a listed protective breaker.
- **ESDPA "green light" prohibition.** The Electric Shock Drowning Prevention
  Association warns against any device marketed as indicating it is "safe to swim." A
  fault can start after someone is already in the water. Positioning must never make a
  swim-safety claim.
- **Patent cluster.** Existing patents around "stray voltage detection for ESD,"
  "dock management with GFCI failure indication," and "shock detector." Freedom-to-
  operate is a real diligence item requiring a proper opinion, not a quick read.
- **Incumbent one firmware sprint away.** MyDockLink (Firstmate Controls) already runs
  a hub + subscription + dealer-channel model in this exact market for boat-lift
  control. Adding leakage monitoring and load control is not a moat they can't cross.
- **Connectivity at the dock is not a given.** The box sits on a structure often
  100–500 ft from the house, over water, with no ethernet, marginal WiFi, and possibly
  no cellular. Remote access is the primary renewal hook, so connectivity is
  load-bearing, not a detail (see Connectivity section).
- **Internet-connected device switching mains relays.** Needs an explicit authN/authZ
  model and remote-actuation safeguards even if hardening is staged.
- Solo / small team, pre-funding.

## Connectivity (load-bearing — the renewal thesis depends on it)

Remote access is the likeliest reason a homeowner keeps paying in year two, and the
box may have no usable network. Options, roughly cheapest to most robust:

- **Homeowner WiFi + a directional bridge / outdoor AP** to the dock: ~$60–150/unit,
  works when the house is close and line-of-sight is clean. Fragile.
- **LTE/Cat-M gateway** in the box: ~$30–60 hardware + ~$3–8/mo data. Adds a recurring
  cost that eats into subscription margin but removes the dependency on the homeowner's
  network. Likely the right default.
- **LoRa/sub-GHz link** dock-to-house bridge for telemetry only (not enough bandwidth
  for the app, fine for alerts): cheap, but then control still needs another path.

**Decision for v1:** assume LTE gateway; fold the data cost into the subscription
model. **Add "dock has verified connectivity" as a pilot-site screening criterion.**

## Feasibility — sensing topology and MCU failsafe (the technical crux)

**This is the question Approach A rests on: can you sense residual current cheaply and
correctly on an existing dock feed with off-the-shelf parts?**

### Residual-current sensing

- **The original idea (clamp CT on the ground conductor) was wrong.** An SCT-013-class
  clamp CT is a 0–100 A *load-current* part. ESD-relevant leakage is **1–30 mA of
  residual current** — the imbalance between the outgoing and returning conductors.
- **Correct method:** a residual-current transformer / monitor (RCM) that encircles
  **all live feed conductors together** (both hots + neutral for split-phase 240 V; hot
  + neutral for 120 V). The sum is zero when there is no leakage; a non-zero sum is
  current going to ground / water.
- **Type A vs Type B:** Type A senses AC and pulsating-DC residuals. A boat charger,
  inverter, or some LED drivers can leak **smooth DC**, which a Type A misses — that is
  Type B territory. Type B RCMs are several times the cost. **EE pass decides this**,
  and the answer changes the BOM materially.
- **The certified ELCI already does the protecting** at 30 mA. The RCM here is a
  *measurement* device feeding the monitor — it never has to be a listed protective
  component, which is what keeps v1 out of the UL cert path.
- **Rough BOM impact (to be priced by the EE):** an off-the-shelf DIN-rail RCM +
  display/relay-output module runs roughly $80–250 (Type A) to $250–600 (Type B),
  versus the ~$10 clamp CT originally assumed. This is the single biggest BOM line and
  it must be nailed before quoting a builder.
- **Open risk:** retrofitting an RCM around the feed conductors of an *existing* dock
  may require re-landing conductors at the panel or a sub-enclosure — added install
  labor that feeds Open Q5.

### MCU / relay failsafe (a networked device switching mains)

- **Default state on boot / brownout / power restoration:** relays default to a
  defined state (recommend: lighting OFF, safety-critical accessory circuits per a
  configured default), never "last unknown state."
- **Watchdog + brownout detection** on the MCU; a hung WiFi/LTE stack must not leave
  relays stuck or block the local safety-monitor loop.
- **Contact-weld / stuck-relay detection:** sense downstream current vs commanded state
  and alert on a mismatch.
- **Connectivity loss:** local control and local alerting continue; the box buffers
  telemetry and flushes on reconnect (Open Q11 covers the security side).
- **Dock power loss:** decide whether the box carries a small battery/supercap to send
  a "power lost" alert before it dies. Likely yes — a silent dead box is
  indistinguishable from a healthy one.

### ELCI trip-state sensing

- **Most certified ELCI breakers have no communications output.** The tile logic and
  the failsafe both assume "the breaker reports a trip," which is not free. Options:
  (a) a breaker with an **auxiliary status contact** (confirm whether the shortlisted
  LineGard PGFM / SmartPlug expose one — if not, this changes the breaker choice), or
  (b) **infer a trip** from downstream current dropping to zero while the box still has
  control power. Add the aux-contact wiring to the kit BOM if (a).

### Pilot-unit environmental survivability

- Marine hardening is deferred to Approach B, but the **pilot on-dock unit still has to
  survive a full season on real water** — freeze/thaw, condensation, salt or lake
  corrosion, driven rain, and a nearby lightning strike. The "listed/rated enclosure"
  line covers ingress; explicitly accept that the pilot unit is not lightning- or
  submersion-proof and that a lost pilot unit is a known, bounded risk.

## Premises

1. **The safety half rides on top of a certified ELCI breaker.** v1 does not build the
   certified protective device — the box is monitoring, event logging, trend alerting,
   compliance record, and the builder diagnostic feed. *Agreed.*
2. **The safety half is not marketed as "safe to swim."** Positioned to the owner as
   electrical-health / peace-of-mind / liability record, and to the builder as fewer
   callbacks + proactive service. *Agreed.*
3. **Builder-first, but multi-pilot — not a single builder.** Cold-interview 8–10
   builders, get **3** to paid pilots, resolve exclusivity before signing any of them,
   and separately test homeowner renewal. Homeowner demand and insurer/regulation
   tailwinds come later. *Agreed (revised from "one builder" after cross-model
   challenge).*
4. **The subscription must deliver ongoing value the owner would miss if it stopped**
   — remote control, monitoring/alerts, service pipeline — not just unlock an app the
   owner already paid hardware for. Local control keeps working without a subscription.
   *Agreed.*

An independent Claude subagent (no view of the conversation, structured summary only)
gave this cold read:

- **Strongest version:** "Monitored dock infrastructure sold through the builder," not

  are what make it recurring revenue.
- **Most revealing fact:** MyDockLink already runs the exact hub + subscription +

  incumbent is close. The defensible thing is the builder relationship and load-control

  version gets eaten.
- **Challenged premise:** the original "lives or dies on ONE builder." A friendly
  builder says yes to anything and proves nothing about willingness-to-pay or a second
  builder's interest; it also hides the real question (do homeowners renew?), and it
  raised the exclusivity trap. → Premise 3 was revised.
- **Bench prototype (~1 week):** instrument a bench rig — a microcontroller + a
  **residual-current sensor around all feed conductors together** (see the Feasibility
  section; the cheap clamp-CT idea in the original was wrong) + a 2–4 channel
  off-the-shelf relay board — publish residual current + per-circuit power over MQTT, a
  small backend on Timescale/Postgres, and one web page with a homeowner view (presets,
  on/off, a "system nominal / anomaly detected" tile that does **not** say "safe to
  swim") and a builder view (one dock, residual-current trend chart, alert log,
  "request service" button). Threshold + trend alerting via email/SMS. Skip: custom
  PCB, enclosure, real dimming, UL/marine hardening, native apps, multi-tenant auth,
  billing, OTA, anything protective.
- **Biggest risk:** nobody renews the subscription. Homeowners pay once for hardware,
  use local control (which works without a subscription by design), and churn after
  year one; a ~$3.75/mo royalty is not enough to make a builder chase renewals.
  Recurring revenue is the whole valuation thesis and the least-evidenced part.
- **Cheapest test this month:** smoke-test landing page + 10 builder calls + 15
  homeowner calls, with a real "$50 deposit / reserve a pilot install" button. Ask
  builders: "Would you install this as standard and tell your client it is a $15/mo
  service?" Ask homeowners with existing high-end docks the year-two renewal question
  directly. Secondary: a preliminary attorney read on the ESD patent cluster.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: Integration layer, monitoring-led (RECOMMENDED — chosen)

Do not manufacture a box yet. Spec a bill of materials from off-the-shelf parts:
certified ELCI breaker (North Shore Safety LineGard / SmartPlug) + a **residual-current
monitor (RCM — Type A or B pending EE review; DC-capable loads like a boat charger or
inverter may force Type B)** + a gateway (microcontroller or Shelly Pro / industrial
relay modules) + **relay channels (no dimming in v1)**. The product is the firmware,
the app, the cloud, the builder portal, and a tight install playbook. Lead with the
safety record + single-dock builder diagnostics view; lighting presets are a
fast-follow on the same relay channels; dimming is added only after demand is proven.

**Platform-ready headroom (added by /plan-ceo-review):** spec the I/O with spare
relay + sensor channels beyond the v1 dock use case, and model the cloud schema
around a generic `device` / `channel` abstraction rather than dock-specific tables.
No other vertical (boat lift, cameras, gate, water level) gets built in v1 — this
is purely so the waterfront-property-OS path later costs a config change, not a
hardware respin and a schema migration. Near-zero cost at spec time.

**Builder service dashboard in v1 (added by /plan-ceo-review):** because the
$3.75/mo royalty is too thin to earn a builder's attention before ~50 docks, the
v1 builder dashboard carries real service-business value on dock #1: a
**service-request queue** (one status field per request — no scheduling, dispatch,
routing, or SLA tracking), **per-dock history** (installs, alerts, visits), and a
**warranty / install log**. A real addition to the v1 software (human ~3-4 wks /
CC ~1 wk), plus a **minimal single-builder login** (email + password, one builder
org, no team roles — this is the auth carve-out that keeps full "builder accounts"
deferred). **Built only if the 30-day demand gate passes.** It is what makes the
builder care before the royalty does.

- Effort: the **bench prototype subset** (firmware + MQTT + one web page + email/SMS
  alerts) is **~1 week** of build with Claude Code. The **plan of record** — a shippable
  v1 — is **~4–6 months of calendar**, dominated not by code but by on-dock testing,
  the install playbook, the EE pass on sensing, and legal (FTO, liability, entity, ToS).
  The v1 builder service dashboard additions (service queue, per-dock history, warranty
  log, minimal single-builder login) add ~1 week CC. The non-prototype software (full
  builder accounts, billing, royalty automation) is a few more weeks of build but is
  deferred (see the "Skip" list in the Cross-Model section and the DEFERRED items in
  the Distribution Plan).
- Risk: Medium.
- Pros: ships in months; tests builder pilots and subscription renewal on real docks
  before hardware capital; software margin; moat becomes the data + builder network.
- Cons: "kit of parts" is a harder builder sell than one clean box; thin-to-no
  hardware margin; custom hardware still needed eventually; quality/UX/support harder
  across mixed third-party components.
- Reuses: certified ELCI hardware, RCM modules, relay/gateway ecosystems, managed MQTT
  + Timescale.

### Approach B: Custom bundled box, full vision (rejected for now)

One marine-hardened SKU: monitoring + 8–12 relay/dimmer channels + presets + app +
subscription + OTA, sold as "the dock electrical package." Rejected as the starting
point: makes the founder a hardware company (custom PCB, enclosure tooling, marine
testing, ~$100k+ capital, 12–18 months) before there is a paying customer, and
validates demand last. This is the destination *after* Approach A proves demand.

### Approach C: Software/service layer + OEM partnership (deferred — gated behind the 30-day demand gate)

Partner with an existing ELCI / marine-power manufacturer (North Shore Safety,
SmartPlug, Blue Sea, a power-pedestal maker) for certified hardware and marine
channel; own the cloud, app, builder network, royalty engine, and diagnostics.
Neutralizes the incumbent-copy risk by making an incumbent the supplier; gets
certified marine hardware with no cert timeline. Downside: no control of the hardware
roadmap, partner leverage is weak pre-traction, dealmaking is slow.
**Gate: do not start Approach C outreach until the 30-day demand gate below passes.**

## Recommended Approach

**Approach A now; Approach C outreach gated behind the 30-day demand gate.**

Rationale: Approach A is the only path that tests the two things most likely to kill
the company — whether builders will actually attach and re-order (revised Premise 3),
and whether the subscription renews (the cross-model biggest risk) — on real docks
before spending hardware capital or handing leverage to a partner. Approach B is the
destination once demand is proven.

## Open Questions

1. **Subscription value and price.** What is behind the paywall (remote access, alerts,
   service pipeline, builder dashboard), and at what price does a builder tell the
   client "it's a $__/mo service" without flinching? How do the first 3 builders get
   paid enough to care about renewals before they hit ~50 docks?
2. **Exclusivity.** Will design-partner builders demand territory exclusivity? What is
   the founder willing to grant, and does granting it collapse the reachable market?
3. **Freedom to operate.** Does the ESD patent cluster block the monitoring/alerting
   approach? Needs a real FTO opinion (10–30+ hours, ~$4–10k) **before any firmware is
   deployed on a real dock** — a 3-hour consult only buys a preliminary verbal read.
   The ~1-week bench rig may proceed on the preliminary read.
4. **Liability posture (now IN SCOPE, staged — /plan-ceo-review).** An alerting product
   in a drowning-adjacent domain can be argued to assume a duty of care; one injury
   suit naming the founder ends a pre-revenue company, disclaimers or not.
   - *30-day window:* form the business entity; open conversations with 2-3
     product-liability brokers and get at least a **written risk-appetite response**.
     Firm quotes routinely lag for a pre-revenue, pre-cert product — a slow quote is
     not a kill; a "we won't insure this" is.
   - *Before the first on-dock unit:* full **counsel-drafted ToS + disclaimer +
     homeowner install sign-off**. Ask counsel specifically whether "trend alerting"
     changes the liability picture versus passive logging.
   - *Pre-entity money:* $50 deposits and builder pilot fees collected before the
     entity exists are held refundable in a dedicated account (or taken as a sole
     proprietor pending formation) — confirm the clean path with counsel.
5. **Install reality: labor, permit, inspection, licensure.** Can a marine electrician
   install the kit in about the time a plain ELCI breaker takes? Installing in the dock
   feed path is electrical work that in many jurisdictions needs a licensed marine
   electrician plus permit and AHJ inspection even with a pre-flashed drop-ship kit —
   this must be in the install playbook and priced.
6. **Homeowner renewal mechanism.** With local control working without a subscription,
   what concretely makes a homeowner keep paying in year two? (Remote access when away
   from the property is the leading hypothesis — needs validation, and depends on the
   Connectivity decision.)
7. **Data ownership and consent.** The builder receives a feed of the homeowner's
   electrical data. Who owns it, what does the homeowner consent to at install, and
   what exactly does the builder see?
8. **Ownership transfer on property sale.** Waterfront homes change hands. Does the
   subscription transfer, lapse, or restart? Does the installer-of-record (and their
   royalty) persist to the new owner?
9. **Builder failure.** If the installing builder goes out of business, who services
   the dock, and what happens to the royalty and the subscription?
10. **Support / warranty / RMA for a kit of mixed third-party parts.** When a
    gateway, RCM, or relay module fails in the field, who owns the support call and the
    replacement?
11. **Security threat model** for a remotely-actuated mains device: authN, authZ,
    command signing, rate limits, and a safe-state on connectivity loss. (The physical
    failsafe requirements — default relay state, watchdog, weld detection — are in the
    Feasibility section; this question is the network/auth side.)
12. **Sensing topology, RCM type, and BOM cost** — the Feasibility section lays out the
    approach and the open Type A vs Type B question; it needs an EE pass to close, and
    the RCM choice swings the BOM by hundreds of dollars per unit.

## Alerting (starting definition for the prototype builder)

- The certified ELCI trips at 30 mA residual current (hardware, not us).
- **Monitoring alert** (email/SMS + dashboard) fires when measured residual current:
  (a) exceeds a fixed floor of **~10 mA**, OR (b) shows a rising 7-day trend that
  crosses **~15 mA** (half the trip threshold), OR (c) makes a rapid step change of
  **>5 mA** between readings.
- These numbers are a starting point for the bench prototype, to be tuned against real
  dock data and reviewed by an EE. They are a *maintenance* signal, never a
  safe-to-swim signal.
- **"System nominal / anomaly detected" tile:** shows "anomaly detected" when any
  alert condition above is active, when the certified breaker reports a trip, or when
  the box loses contact with a sensor. Otherwise "nominal." The tile never implies the
  water is safe.

## Distribution Plan

- **Certified ELCI breaker sourcing:** for the pilot / prototype units the **founder
  specs and buys** the breaker (it's in the 90-day budget, and vendor selection is a
  Dependency). In **production**, the builder sources it through normal
  marine-electrical channels and the founder's kit excludes it — the kit is the parts a
  builder can't easily get right on their own.
- **v1 kit (integration layer):** the founder drop-ships a kit (gateway, RCM, relay
  modules, LTE gateway, wiring harness, rated enclosure, install playbook). Firmware
  ships pre-flashed. App is a web app first (no app-store review latency), native later.
- **Cloud:** managed MQTT broker + a small backend service + Timescale/Postgres on a
  single cloud VM to start.
- **Updates:** OTA deferred to post-validation; v1 units updated on a service visit.
- **CI/CD:** GitHub Actions building the firmware image and deploying the backend on
  merge; manual kit assembly until volume justifies a contract assembler.
- **Billing + royalty (DEFERRED):** no paying subscriber exists for 90+ days and no
  renewal for a year. For pilots, **track subscriptions and royalties in a
  spreadsheet.** Build the Stripe integration, builder accounts, and automated royalty
  payout only after the first real renewal proves the model.
